\title{\textbf{Auditing the Winner:\\[2pt] Critic Disagreement Adds No Detectable Failure Signal\\ Beyond the Selector on SWE-Agent Trajectories}\\[6pt]

\date{\small September 27, 2026}

\begin{abstract}
When an agent commits the best of $n$ attempts under a critic, the committed trajectory is where the critic's confidence and its errors coincide. A common proposal is to audit it with a disjoint panel of critics and escalate on disagreement. We pre-registered and ran this test with executed gold on SWE-bench Verified (41 instances, 9 repositories) and SWE-bench Multilingual (114 instances, 37 repositories): best-of-4 selection by a reward model, a checkpoint-disjoint panel, and the official test harness. Silent failures --- committed, agent-declared-complete trajectories that fail hidden tests --- were 35\% and 45\% of the population. On the registered endpoint --- the out-of-fold gain in AUROC from adding panel disagreement to the selector's own score --- disagreement adds nothing detectable: pooled over 47 failures and 46 repositories, the three estimators that use every row give $-0.03$, $-0.01$ and $+0.01$, every interval includes zero, and the loosest pooled upper bound is $+0.12$. Disagreement alone ranks failures at AUROC 0.51 and 0.57, indistinguishable from the selecting critic's own score, and this held across 4k--16k contexts, pruned overlapping windows, eight reward models from 1B to 32B including two code-trained ones, two re-selection regimes, and a single-family panel of LLM judges. Point estimates that cleared a structural baseline on Verified (windowed disagreement 0.69; patch size 0.67; one 1B critic 0.71) all fell to chance on 37 repositories: no configuration produced a replicable signal. An unselected-candidate control shows that with this selector the committed candidate fails no more often than one passed over, while the selector's own score ranks failures at 0.81 among unselected candidates and 0.61 among committed ones on Verified --- the selection-conditional gap measured directly. Re-selecting with \texttt{gpt-5}, which ranks pool failures at 0.71 on both benchmarks, picks better than chance where the primary selector did not, and re-commits three-quarters of the population, does not reverse this: on 101 rows and 44 failures $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$ is 0.591 and three estimators of the endpoint give $+0.012$, $-0.024$ and $-0.018$ [$-0.11$, $+0.07$] --- mixed signs, none large, none with a degenerate baseline. We report that cell as \emph{consistent with} the null rather than establishing it, because the upper bound of $+0.073$ still exceeds the $+0.05$ this project declared actionable. Test--retest shows the critics are stable ($r\geq0.98$) and content-driven: the failure is in what disagreement measures. On a capable open-weight agent the question could not be posed: \texttt{DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash} fails 0.200 of 456 attempts against 0.579 for \texttt{gpt-5-mini} at the same budget, yet no critic separates its failures from its successes above chance --- the \texttt{gpt-5} judge at 0.552 and the five reward models scored on this axis at 0.419--0.523 against a registered selector bar of 0.65 --- so on strong agents the critic, not the disagreement, is what fails first. We argue that audit signals must be evaluated on the population the selector commits, against the selector's own score, with executed gold; we report an upper bound on the residual value of disagreement; and we document the instrument failures that would otherwise have produced a positive result.
\end{abstract}

\begin{center}\fbox{\begin{minipage}{0.95\linewidth}\small
\textbf{In plain terms.} A coding agent tries a task several times and a scoring model picks its best attempt. We checked those picks against the project's real test suite. A third to a half of them were broken --- the agent said it was finished, the scorer agreed, and the tests failed. The idea we tested was a safety net: ask several other scoring models to grade the same attempt, and treat \emph{their disagreement} as a warning that something is wrong. On the trajectories this agent produced, that safeguard did not yield a replicable signal beyond the original scorer. Disagreement is usually near a coin flip, and on the registered endpoint its incremental value is indistinguishable from zero with a loosest pooled upper bound of $+0.12$. The scoring models themselves are not the problem --- they are consistent, and the best of them spots about four in five of the failures on its own. The problem is that measuring how much they \emph{differ} throws that information away. We also show why this is easy to get wrong: several promising-looking results in our own pilot disappeared when we re-ran everything on three times as many code repositories, and seven bugs in our own measuring equipment would each have produced a convincing but false finding. A stronger agent remains untested.
\end{minipage}}\end{center}

\section{Introduction}
Best-of-$n$ selection under a learned critic is now a default in agent scaffolds: generate several trajectories, score each with a reward model, commit the argmax. Three claims about this are easy to conflate, and this paper keeps them apart. The \emph{theory} is that argmax selection on a noisy score induces the optimizer's curse~\citep{smith2006optimizer}: among candidates, the top-scored one is disproportionately the one whose flaws the critic cannot see, so the score carries less information about the committed candidate than about the pool. What we \emph{observe} is one instance of that theory measured directly: on SWE-bench Verified the selecting critic ranks failures at 0.81 among the candidates it passed over and at 0.61 among the ones it committed, on the same instances. What we do \emph{not} show is that selection raised the failure rate of the committed candidate --- with the selector we used, it did not (\S\ref{sec:control}). A committed trajectory that fails its task while carrying a high score is a \emph{silent failure}; they are a third to a half of what this agent committed, whether or not selection put them there.

The obvious safeguard is a second opinion --- or several. If a panel of critics, disjoint from the selecting critic, disagrees sharply about a committed trajectory, that disagreement might carry information the selecting critic has exhausted. The proposal behind this work stated the hypothesis as a falsifiable, selection-conditional estimand and committed in advance to publishing the null.

This paper reports that result. The idea that correlated critics share blind spots is not ours: the correlated-judge literature predicts it~\citep{goel2025great,kim2025correlated,kohli2026nine,hossain2026agreement,afrin2026ensembles}, and best-of-$n$ reward hacking is well characterized~\citep{gao2023scaling,khalaf2025inference,yu2026caution}. What had not been done is the measurement: put a disjoint audit panel on the population a critic actually committed, with executed gold, structural baselines and pre-registered bars, and ask what it adds over the selector. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{A selection-conditional evaluation protocol for audit signals} --- selected population, selector baseline, executed gold, pre-registered bars --- with its first two-benchmark instantiation on SWE-agent trajectories. Candidate-pool discrimination does not measure residual auditing value on the selected population~\citep{emadi2026scaling,trilemma2026}; we evaluate on the selected population directly.
\item \textbf{An upper bound on what disagreement can be worth, not a demonstration that it is worthless.} Forty-seven positives cannot establish equivalence and we do not claim it. On the proposal's registered endpoint --- out-of-fold AUROC of a model with the selector's score and panel disagreement over one with the score alone --- disagreement's incremental value is indistinguishable from zero under four estimators of it, with a loosest pooled upper bound of $+0.12$ over 47 positives and 46 repositories, and $+0.25$ on the smaller benchmark alone (\S\ref{sec:endpoint}). The primary protocol's own upper interval is $+0.22$; across the whole configuration sweep the widest reaches $+0.63$, on the code-trained critic's 16k re-selection over eleven positives; positives that appeared on twelve Verified failures did not travel.
\item \textbf{Diagnostics and an instrument record:} test--retest stability of long-window reward-model scores; the repository-clustering ceiling on effective $n$ for SWE-bench Verified; and seven instrument failures that each produced a plausible false finding.
\end{itemize}
Throughout, ``critic'' means an off-the-shelf reward model or LLM judge used without task-specific training. Trained trajectory verifiers~\citep{shum2025swerm,rahman2026scout,wang2026failfast} are a different class; we return to them in \S\ref{sec:carry}.

\section{Setup and estimand}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_design.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{How the experiment works.} An agent writes several candidate fixes; a scoring model picks one; a separate panel grades the pick; the project's real tests decide whether it was right. $c$ is the selecting critic's scalar score of a whole trajectory; best-of-$n$ commits the argmax. A disjoint panel of $m$ reward models produces $u$, the sample standard deviation of their rank-normalized scores (rank norms fit on training repositories only). Gold $g$ is the official SWE-bench harness verdict on the committed patch.}\label{fig:design}\end{figure}

\paragraph{Unit and gold.} The unit is one committed trajectory $\tau$. Gold $g(\tau)\in\{0,1\}$ is trajectory-level task success under the official \texttt{swebench} 5.x harness; failed tests do not imply every step was wrong, and we make no step-level claims. Positive class throughout is failure ($\gzero$).

\paragraph{Selection and audit.} $c_{\mathrm{sel}}$ scores the full committed trajectory text; best-of-$n$ ranks $n$ full candidates by this score and commits the top-1. The audit panel $\{c_1,\ldots,c_m\}$ satisfies $c_{\mathrm{sel}}\notin\{c_i\}$ at checkpoint level; $u$ is their disagreement.

\paragraph{Primary population.} Committed trajectories whose agent exited with a submission and a non-empty patch --- the agent believed it was done. Within it, silent failures are $\gzero$.

\paragraph{Metrics.} M1: $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$ for silent failure. M2: $\mathrm{AUROC}(-c)$. M3 $= $ M1 $-$ M2 with a repository-clustered bootstrap (4{,}000 resamples, groups resampled with the fitted scores held fixed). A held-out logistic $\DeltaAUROC$ was pre-specified as confirmatory only when $\geq 10$ positives fall in the test split; with 41 instances it never did, and we report raw-score AUROCs as registered.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} Population, positive class, metrics, predictions and a stopping rule (``fewer than ten positives yields no conclusion in either direction'') were committed to the repository before any gold existed (\texttt{PREREGISTRATION\_SWEBENCH\_PILOT.md}). Each later analysis was added as a dated amendment before its data existed. Every deviation is declared in \S\ref{sec:limits}.

\section{Instrument, and what it took to trust it}\label{sec:instrument}
The pilot runs on a laptop and free GPU tiers: mini-swe-agent 2.4.6 driving \texttt{gpt-5-mini} through the OpenAI API (\$0.02 per trajectory with prompt caching), SWE-bench images under Rosetta in a Colima VM on Apple Silicon, reward models on Metal or on Kaggle's free T4s, and the official harness for gold.

We record seven instrument failures because each would have produced a publishable-looking result, and each was caught by an oracle control, a regression test, or a registered audit of the pipeline before a number was trusted.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
\item A sandbox flag hid \texttt{numpy} from the gold harness: every trajectory scored as a failure. Caught because the benchmark's reference solutions are run through the harness before any model patch.
\item An LLM-as-judge selecting critic emitted three distinct values over 640 candidates, so best-of-16 silently became first-draw: 39 of 40 committed trajectories were identical to the $n{=}4$ run. Selection had not happened in the earlier pilots. Caught by the chosen-index histogram.
\item On a fresh VM the harness's image pulls failed on an architecture manifest: 41/41 failures in 499 seconds. Caught by the harness's own error count.
\item Our power function reported $\approx$0.40 ``power'' under the true null. Caught by simulating the null; replaced by a null-calibrated clustered bootstrap.
\item Repository grouping truncated hyphenated repository names, corrupting the held-out split. Caught by a regression test on the identifier parser.
\item Rank normalization was fit on all rows, leaking test rows into the panel statistic. Caught by a test that fits on the training split only. Every $u$ reported in this paper is post-fix: recomputing the stored values from raw panel scores with training-repository normalization reproduces them exactly (max absolute difference $0.0$ on both benchmarks), while the leaky computation differs by up to $0.023$ and would have given $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)=0.523$ rather than 0.508 on Verified.
\item The selector-axis analysis filtered its population by the \emph{previous} selector's committed candidate, not the new one's, silently dropping 16 eligible instances on Multilingual. It produced $\DeltaAUROC = +0.375$ [$+0.21$, $+0.52$] from a baseline collapsed to 0.150 --- a result large enough to reverse this paper --- which on the correct population is $+0.012$ from a sound baseline. Caught by asking whether the eligibility flag existed per candidate, and by the four-estimator rule that made the anomaly visible in the first place (\S\ref{sec:selector}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}
\subsection{Silent failures are common among committed trajectories}\label{sec:control}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_pilot.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Where the sample goes, and why it is small.} Top left: best-of-4 with a true reward-model critic genuinely selected (chosen index spread across all four slots; an earlier LLM-judge critic had collapsed to index 0). Top right: from 164 candidates to 12 silent failures. Bottom left: per-repository failure rates, 9 repositories, ICC 0.18. Bottom right: effective sample size on SWE-bench Verified as a function of repository ICC; the public estimate from six leaderboard submissions is 0.033--0.053.}\label{fig:pilot}\end{figure}

Fifty instances were sampled with a fixed seed; generation completed for 41 (the deviation is explained in \S\ref{sec:limits}). Of 164 candidates, 138 exited having submitted (15.9\% hit the 40-step limit); 17.7\% produced no patch at all and are excluded. Selection by \texttt{Skywork-Reward-V2-Qwen3-4B} committed 34 usable trajectories; the harness resolved 22 and failed 12, with zero errors.

The registration named a go/no-go decision as the pilot's actual output, requiring at least ten silent failures projected for a confirmatory run, an exclusion rate at or below 40\%, and no ceiling effect in the selector's held-out score. It resolved \textbf{GO} with no binding reason: 146 silent failures projected at 500 instances, a 17.7\% exclusion rate, and the headroom check not applicable for want of ten held-out positives. That decision is what licensed the replication. \textbf{35.3\% [21.5, 52.1] of committed, agent-declared-complete trajectories were silent failures} on Verified, and 45.5\% on Multilingual (\S\ref{sec:replication}). On HumanEval+ the same pipeline yielded 8\% at any selection pressure; SWE-bench supplies the phenomenon in quantity. Selection was real (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot}, top left), and $\mathrm{corr}(c,u)=0.11$, so $u$ was near-orthogonal to $c$ --- the precondition the design wants. Whether selection \emph{concentrated} failure --- whether the committed candidate fails more often than one the critic did not pick --- is a separate question that a committed-only sample cannot answer; Amendment 4f, pre-registered before grading, answers it with two named predictions: one unselected candidate per instance was graded on both benchmarks (31 and 71 paired rows). \textbf{F-P1}, that selection concentrates failure, \emph{failed on both}. \textbf{F-P2}, that the selector sees more on the random arm than on the committed one by more than 0.10, \emph{passed on Verified and failed on Multilingual}; the asymmetry below is therefore a registered prediction that replicated on neither benchmark as a pair, and we report it as one result on one benchmark rather than as a general effect. \textbf{The committed candidate failed no more often than one the critic passed over}: $-3$ points on Verified [$-21$, $0$] and $-4$ on Multilingual [$-11$, $+3$]. With this selector, best-of-4 did not concentrate failure, and we claim a failure \emph{rate}, not concentration. \textbf{F-P3} registered no prediction and required only that disagreement on the random arm be reported: it is $\mathrm{AUROC}(u) = 0.367$ on Verified and 0.51 on Multilingual. Both sit at or below chance, so the range-restriction reading F-P3 describes --- disagreement working on an unselected pool while failing on the committed one --- is not available: there is no signal on the random arm either. The control also measures the selection-conditional gap directly. On Verified the selector's own score ranks failures among the candidates it did \emph{not} pick at 0.81 [0.74, 1.00], but among the ones it committed at 0.61 on the same instances: selection consumed the critic's own signal, which is the premise of the design. On Multilingual the critic has no signal in either arm (0.43 unselected, 0.51 committed), so there was nothing for selection to remove. Panel disagreement on the unselected arm is 0.37 and 0.51 --- no better off-selection than on (Fig.~\ref{fig:control}).

\subsection{What the failures are: a test-derived taxonomy}\label{sec:taxonomy}
A null invites the question of whether it averages over classes, one of which disagreement does detect. The harness answers this without asking a model: for every committed trajectory it records which \texttt{FAIL\_TO\_PASS} tests the patch turned green and which \texttt{PASS\_TO\_PASS} tests it broke. Five mutually exclusive classes exhaust the 47 silent failures (Table~\ref{tab:taxonomy}). Nearly two thirds are the dullest kind: the agent edited something, declared the task finished, broke nothing, and moved not one target test. A sixth broke previously passing tests without fixing the bug. Five cases, all on Multilingual, fixed the target and broke something else --- the only class where the agent did the work the task asked for and the accounting still records a failure.

Disagreement detects none of them. Only one class clears the pre-registered inference floor of ten positives --- no progress, on Multilingual --- and there $\mathrm{AUROC}(u) = 0.534$ [0.367, 0.697] against the passing trajectories. The rest hold one to eight positives and range from 0.438 to 0.818, the top of that range being a single instance whose patch did not apply, with no ordering that survives a glance at the intervals; we report them descriptively and draw nothing from them. The pooled null is not a mixture of a detectable subclass and an undetectable one.

One incidental measurement belongs here, because it bounds what \emph{any} auditor on this benchmark can be checked against. The median analysed instance is guarded by exactly one \texttt{FAIL\_TO\_PASS} test, and 84 of 111 analysed rows are guarded by exactly one: 22 of 34 on Verified and 62 of 77 on Multilingual. ``Resolved'' is usually a single test's verdict, so the label our AUROCs are computed against is thinner than the word suggests.

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} and Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} are the core of the paper. The primary analysis --- four chat-preference reward models auditing a 4{,}096-token prefix --- gave $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)=0.508$. Every subsequent amendment was declared before its data existed and asked whether some specific limitation of that primary was hiding the signal:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \emph{The critics never saw the patch.} Scoring the final diff plus the last 3{,}000 tokens: $u$ fell to 0.458.
\item \emph{The critics were too small, or the wrong ones.} Six chat-preference RMs from 1B to 8B, including the proposal's named critic \texttt{Skywork-Reward-V2-\allowbreak Llama-3.1-8B}, each scored with the patch in view: as individual detectors of the 12 known silent failures they scored 0.45--0.64, four of six at or below chance; the panel gave 0.494.
\item \emph{Selection was by the wrong critic.} Re-selecting with the named 8B critic changed 20 of 34 committed trajectories; graded, the failure rate was again 35\%, and $u$ was 0.455. Repeating the re-selection at a true 16k changed 23 of 33: $u$ 0.483, while the critic's own score on the set it had itself selected reached 0.736.
\item \emph{Chat-preference training is the problem.} \texttt{AceCodeRM-7B}, trained on code with execution-feedback preferences, scored \textbf{0.405} as a detector --- below chance --- and adding it to the panel left $u$ at 0.473. One registered prediction attached to this critic, A2-P3, asked whether its prefix and full-length scores correlate above 0.5, as a critic with a stable view of quality should. \textbf{It is not evaluable}: the model fell back to a 4k window on 31 of the 34 committed rows even on a 24\,GB GPU, so its apparent $r=0.997$ is a score correlated with itself rather than a prefix-versus-full contrast, and its 32B sibling was run only at 8k/16k and has no prefix pass. We report the prediction as attempted and undecidable, and give the selector's own contrast instead: $r=0.35$ between its 4k prefix and its true 16k score, and $r=0.18$ between the prefix and the patch-in-view window. As a selector it changed 24 of 34 trajectories; $u$ on that set was 0.542, its own score 0.472. Its 32B sibling, run in fp16 on an 80\,GB GPU (131 of 164 rows at 8k, the rest at 16k), scored 0.557 as a detector and 0.50 by mean window.
\item \emph{The context was too short.} Re-scored on a 24\,GB GPU at a true 16k tokens (every row at the declared length for the six chat RMs; the code-trained model fell back to 4k on 153/164 rows), five of six chat RMs improved as individual detectors --- the 1B Skywork model to 0.712 --- with paired 4k$\to$16k gains of $+0.04$ to $+0.12$ (the sixth, $-0.03$) whose intervals all include zero. The selecting critic re-scored at 16k reaches 0.587 on its own; Table~\ref{tab:ladder} keeps M2 at its prefix value of 0.621 because that is the score selection actually used and the basis of M3. The panel's disagreement at 16k was 0.439, the lowest of any row: \textbf{A2-P2}, which required the full-length panel to exceed the prefix panel's 0.508 by more than 0.10, \emph{fails}: the full-length panel is 0.169 below its bar, and below the prefix panel it was meant to beat. An 8k point shows the ladder is not monotone: the 8k score sits between the 4k and 16k values for only two of the seven critics that have a score at all three lengths (the 32B has no 4k pass).
\item \emph{Reward models are the wrong class.} Three LLM judges (\texttt{gpt-5}, \texttt{gpt-5-mini}, \texttt{gpt-5-nano}; one vendor, hence a single-family panel) rated the probability that the patch resolves the issue. Shown the problem and the diff, \texttt{gpt-5} reached 0.720 and \texttt{gpt-5-mini} 0.752 --- above the structural bar --- but their disagreement with \texttt{gpt-5-nano} was 0.528. Adding the last 6k tokens of the transcript, including the agent's own test runs, did not help (best judge $-0.03$; disagreement 0.640). \texttt{gpt-5-mini} is also the agent, a self-evaluation confound; \texttt{gpt-5} is not, and scored 0.72.
\end{itemize}

Two registered bars were cleared here and we disclose both, because neither supports the hypothesis and reporting only the failures would be selective. The primary hypothesis P1 was registered as the bare inequality $\mathrm{AUROC}(u) > 0.5$, and $u$ is 0.508 on Verified and 0.565 on Multilingual, so P1 passes as written. We do not treat that as a detector: the bar carries no margin, both intervals include chance, and the paired comparison against the selector is $-0.114$ and $+0.005$. Registering a threshold with no minimum effect size was a design error on our part, and overruling it now is a post-hoc judgement, which we label as one rather than leave the pass unmentioned. A4c-P1, the bar for the best single LLM judge, also passed at 0.752 against 0.665; it concerns a judge's own score rather than disagreement, and the registered reading that a pass narrows the paper's claim to reward-model critics is addressed in \S\ref{sec:limits}. Two registered predictions about critics' \emph{own} scores, not disagreement, also fail and we name them: \textbf{P2} (Goodhart) required $\mathrm{AUROC}(-c)\le 0.5$ and fails at 0.621 --- the selector is not Goodharted on this population --- and \textbf{A2-P1} required AceCodeRM-7B as a critic to exceed 0.60 and fails at 0.405 as a detector (0.472 as a selector). \emph{Every registered prediction about panel disagreement carrying signal beyond the selector failed.} The intervals on M3 are wide --- twelve positives --- and a moderate positive effect is not excluded by any one row; the rows are also re-measurements of the same twelve positives, not independent replications. What they establish jointly is narrower than ``no detector'': under every variation of input length, size, identity, training signal, critic class and selection regime, \emph{the disagreement of a panel on one window of a committed trajectory} showed no detectable information about hidden failure beyond the selecting critic's own score in these samples.

\subsection{Windowed auditing: the one row that clears the bar}\label{sec:windows}

Amendment 3, pre-registered before any windowed score existed, made one structural change: collapse tool outputs longer than 24 lines to their first and last 8, then score overlapping 3{,}500-token windows (25\% overlap, problem statement prefixed, final window anchored at the patch) and take the panel's maximum disagreement. All three registered predictions passed: \textbf{A3-P1}, $\mathrm{AUROC}(u_{\max})=0.686$ against a bar of 0.665; \textbf{A3-P2}, a gain of $+0.18$ over the prefix panel against a bar of $+0.10$; and the named 8B critic's mean-window score, 0.652, against 0.634.

We report this as the study's strongest positive signal and immediately bound it. A free permutation of labels gives $p=0.04$; permuting within repository, which removes the contribution of repositories that both fail more and disagree more, gives $p=0.21$. Leaving out any one of the 34 instances moves the estimate between 0.657 and 0.731 --- across the bar. The paired gain over the selecting critic is $+0.06$ [$-0.11$, $+0.33$]. Pruning removed 69\% of tokens, so most trajectories fit in one or two windows (median two); ``multi-window'' here mostly means ``the pruned trajectory, plus one overlapping cut''. In 43\% of multi-window rows the maximum fell on a window other than the last, so the middle of the trajectory contributes something. $u_{\max}$ is also a maximum of noisy statistics and is biased upward under the null. A reader who takes 0.686 as a detector is reading more than twelve positives can support; a reader who dismisses it is ignoring the one configuration whose pre-registered bars it cleared. Other registered predictions also passed --- the premise check, the LLM-judge single-detector bar, and the replication's null-replication bar --- but this is the only one asserting that \emph{disagreement} detects failure.

\subsection{What the critics do and do not carry}\label{sec:carry}
On Verified, single critics given enough of the solution reached 0.71--0.75 (the 1B reward model at 16k; \texttt{gpt-5} and \texttt{gpt-5-mini} with the patch in view), while every disagreement statistic built on the same scores landed at 0.44--0.64. On Multilingual the reward-model half of that observation did not survive (\S\ref{sec:replication}), and the LLM-judge half shrank: with the patch in view, \texttt{gpt-5} 0.662, \texttt{gpt-5-mini} 0.604, \texttt{gpt-5-nano} 0.576 --- each within its interval of the selecting critic --- and their disagreement 0.514. The strongest single detector among the \texttt{gpt-5}-generation critics is a frontier judge reading the diff, at two-thirds (a newer generation does better, below); aside from the pilot's windowed $u_{\max}=0.686$, which failed to replicate, no disagreement statistic clears chance with an interval excluding 0.5 on either benchmark. Whatever the critics carry, disagreement is not extracting it. A later, exploratory check sharpens this: adding two judges from a newer model generation (\texttt{gpt-6-luna}, \texttt{gpt-6-sol}) raises the best single detector in the study to 0.776 [0.68, 0.87] on the replication and 0.826 [0.77, 1.00] on the pilot --- both beat every \texttt{gpt-5} judge on both benchmarks --- while the disagreement of that newer pair falls to 0.41 on both, \emph{below} the weaker trio's 0.51--0.53, and a five-judge panel gives 0.44 on 35 positives. Better critics; no better disagreement. The gap widens rather than closes as the individual judges improve. Those judges also rank considerably better than they know: every one is overconfident, stating resolve probabilities averaging 0.66--0.84 against actual rates of 0.55--0.65, with expected calibration error of 0.16--0.34 and several Brier scores at or worse than the 0.25 obtained by always answering ``50\%''. Ranking and calibration are not the same axis and neither can be read off the other: the best ranker is \texttt{gpt-6-sol}, whose calibration error on the replication (0.185) is the second \emph{best} of the five, while the largest error belongs to \texttt{gpt-5-mini} (0.338), a middling ranker. Anyone wanting a probability rather than a ranking would have to recalibrate first.

Two things we did not test bound the claim from above. First, the selector is a 4B chat-preference reward model, not a deployed-grade trajectory verifier; whether a stronger selector leaves a different residual for an auditor was open when the study was designed, and \S\ref{sec:selector} addresses it with \texttt{gpt-5}, which changes the result's precision but not its direction; whether a purpose-built trajectory verifier would is still open, and \citet{shum2025swerm} provide the natural instrument. Second, \emph{trained} auditors exist and report better numbers on related populations: a 4B verifier trained on labelled failures selects among candidate trajectories and outperforms frontier judges at finding them~\citep{rahman2026scout}; a 0.6B prefix monitor predicts SWE-agent failure at a 5\% false-positive rate~\citep{wang2026failfast}; lightweight statistical detectors beat LLM judges on agent-claimed false success~\citep{advani2026silent}. Our result is about what a panel of \emph{off-the-shelf} critics contributes through disagreement, evaluated the way an escalation trigger would be used; it says nothing against training an auditor, and the protocol here is how such an auditor should be evaluated. Second, a pre-registered incremental-value test (Amendment 4a) asked whether $u$ or $c$ adds anything to a structural model of the diff and transcript under leave-one-repository-out evaluation: $u$ adds $-0.03$ [$-0.08$, $+0.04$]; $c$ adds $+0.02$ [$-0.10$, $+0.21$]. We declare one defect in that amendment: the registered ``difficulty'' control, the fraction of six public leaderboard runs that solved the instance, turned out to predict our silent failures at 0.95 on its own --- one of those runs is our own agent model, and the others fail the same instances --- so the registered model was at ceiling and uninformative. The numbers above are from the labelled structure-only sensitivity variant; all three variants are in the record.

\subsection{A selector that ranks failures: the regime the proposal named}\label{sec:selector}
The study's largest stated limitation was that a 4B chat reward model barely selects. Amendment 5, pre-registered with fixed bars, re-ran the protocol with \texttt{gpt-5} scoring \emph{every} candidate in both pools and committing its argmax. No new trajectories; 620 candidates re-scored for \$13, and the 59 newly committed patches graded by the same harness.

\textbf{A defect found in review, and what it changed.} The numbers below are not the ones an earlier version of this paper reported, and the reason is a coding error we found while auditing. Amendment 5 re-selects a new committed candidate with \texttt{gpt-5}, but the analysis filtered its population using the \emph{4B critic's} committed candidate's agent-declared-complete flag, which for three-quarters of instances describes a different trajectory. The registered population is committed, agent-declared-complete trajectories, so for this arm the flag must come from the \texttt{gpt-5}-committed candidate; the scoring bundle carries it per candidate. On Verified the two readings give an identical population. On Multilingual the published reading dropped 16 instances whose \texttt{gpt-5}-committed trajectory was complete, non-empty and graded, purely because the 4B critic's pick had been empty. Amendment 2's re-selection rows were checked for the same defect and are unaffected. The corrected analysis is in \S\ref{sec:limits} as a declared deviation and the correction itself is dated in the pre-registration.

\textbf{S-P1 passes on both benchmarks, and on both of its registered conditions.} Across the candidate pool \texttt{gpt-5} ranks failing candidates below passing ones at 0.712 [0.62, 0.81] on Verified and 0.706 [0.63, 0.78] on Multilingual, against the 4B model's 0.81 and 0.43; the pool statistic is computed over graded candidates and is untouched by the population defect. S-P1's second condition asks whether the arm it commits actually fails less often than a candidate drawn at random, and earlier versions of this paper reported only the first. Paired within instance, the committed failure rate is 0.258 against 0.323 on Verified ($-0.065$ [$-0.20$, $0.00$], 31 pairs) and 0.421 against 0.505 on Multilingual ($-0.084$ [$-0.14$, $-0.03$], 95 pairs), with no pair on either benchmark where the committed trajectory failed and the random one passed. Two qualifications: 7 of 31 and 36 of 95 pairs are structural zeros because both arms happened to choose the same candidate, and restricting to pairs where the picks differ gives $-0.083$ [$-0.27$, $0.00$] and $-0.136$ [$-0.22$, $-0.06$]. Earlier text here claimed this was a condition the primary 4B selector failed. That was wrong, and we correct it: the 4B selector also committed candidates that failed less often than random, by $-0.032$ [$-0.21$, $0.00$] and $-0.042$ [$-0.11$, $+0.03$]. The difference between the selectors is one of degree and precision, not direction. What the 4B selector failed was the \emph{opposite} prediction, F-P1, that selection concentrates failure. Where \texttt{gpt-5} is genuinely in a regime the 4B never entered is the pool statistic on Multilingual, 0.706 against 0.43.

\textbf{S-P2 fails on both benchmarks.} The registered bar is a pool-minus-committed gap in $\mathrm{AUROC}$ above 0.10. On Verified the committed score is 0.792, \emph{above} the pool's 0.712, on nine positives and below our inference floor. On Multilingual the corrected gap is 0.706 to 0.671, or 0.035, where the defective population gave 0.115 and a pass. We therefore withdraw the claim that selection measurably consumes a strong selector's own signal. What survives is the narrower observation on the primary 4B selector on Verified, 0.81 among candidates it rejected against 0.61 among those it committed (\S\ref{sec:control}), which is one selector on one benchmark and twelve positives.

\textbf{S-P3 fails on its first condition.} S-P3 required both $\mathrm{AUROC}(u) > 0.60$ and a registered endpoint above $+0.05$ with an interval excluding zero. On the corrected Multilingual population (101 rows, 44 failures, 40 repositories) $\mathrm{AUROC}(u) = 0.591$ [0.47, 0.70], below the bar, so the conjunction fails on its own terms with no appeal to estimator validity. The endpoint is small under every estimator: $+0.012$ from the leave-one-repository-out logistic, $-0.024$ from five clustered folds, $-0.018$ [$-0.11$, $+0.07$] from the unfitted estimator. The signs still disagree; what is new is that none is large and none has a degenerate baseline.

\textbf{And the most alarming number in the study dissolves.} On the defective population the registered leave-one-repository-out logistic reported $\DeltaAUROC = +0.375$ [$+0.21$, $+0.52$] --- large, apparently significant, and enough to reverse the paper --- against a baseline $M_c$ that had collapsed to 0.150 out of fold while the raw $\mathrm{AUROC}$ was 0.591. We had reported that as a degenerate-baseline artefact and overruled it. On the corrected population the same estimator gives $+0.012$ with a baseline of 0.575 against a raw 0.671. The anomaly was the population defect, not a pathology of the estimator. We keep the four-estimator standard regardless, because it is what made the anomaly visible in the first place, and because the Verified cell still shows a depressed logistic baseline (0.669 against a raw 0.792).

With a selector that demonstrably ranks failures and picks better than chance where the primary selector did not, panel disagreement shows no detectable gain, and on the corrected population it also falls below the registered detection bar. The upper bound remains the honest limit: the unfitted estimator's interval reaches $+0.073$, above the $+0.05$ this project declared worth acting on, so this cell is \emph{consistent with} the null and does not establish it. It is inconclusive as to equivalence, and clear as to the registered criteria, all three of which now resolve without any post-hoc overrule.

\subsection{The registered endpoint: does $u$ add to $c$ out of fold?}\label{sec:endpoint}
The proposal's confirmatory quantity was the held-out $\DeltaAUROC$ of a logistic model $M_{c+u}$ over $M_c$. The 70/30 repository split never held ten test positives, so it was never eligible on Verified alone. With 47 positives across both benchmarks it can be computed by leave-one-repository-out, which we declare as a substitution of the cross-validation scheme, not of the estimand.

A two-parameter model refit on eight to thirty-six clusters is a fragile thing to hang a bound on, so we compute the endpoint four ways (Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}). Only the first was pre-registered; the other three are post-hoc robustness checks of it, declared as such, and they exist because the first proved fragile: pooled out-of-fold probabilities from a leave-one-repository-out logistic; the same with five clustered folds; AUROC computed \emph{inside} each fold and averaged by fold size, which avoids pooling probabilities across folds with different calibration; and a fixed unit-weight sum of the two standardized scores, which fits no coefficients at all. The four disagree in sign --- pooled, they give $-0.119$, $-0.030$, $-0.014$ and $+0.013$ --- and we therefore do not report any one of them as the estimate. What they agree on is the conclusion: every interval includes zero. The bound needs stating carefully. Among the three estimators that use every row, \textbf{the loosest pooled upper bound is $+0.12$}; per benchmark it is $+0.25$ (Verified, fixed weights), where twelve positives buy little precision. The fourth estimator, which computes AUROC inside each held-out repository, is the weakest: a single repository is usually all-pass or all-fail, so no AUROC is defined there and the fold is dropped --- 15 of 46 folds and 53 of 111 rows survive, and its interval, $[-0.31, +0.28]$ pooled, excludes almost nothing. We report it for completeness and do not lean on it. The defensible statement is that adding disagreement to the selector's score produces no detectable gain under any of the four estimators we tested, with a pooled upper bound of $+0.12$ from those that use all the data.

The spread itself is instructive. The fixed-weight variant reproduces the raw $\mathrm{AUROC}(-c)$ almost exactly (0.559 vs.\ 0.560 pooled), while the logistic variants report $M_c$ below it (0.48--0.50): pooling out-of-fold probabilities across folds whose intercepts differ scrambles the global ranking, which depresses both arms roughly equally and cancels in the difference. Readers who prefer an assumption-free statement should take the fixed-weight row: $+0.013$ [$-0.07$, $+0.12$] pooled. The discrepancy is worth one sentence beyond this paper: with rank metrics and few clusters, pooling out-of-fold predictions can make a real signal appear to vanish when what has vanished is comparability across refits, and an unfitted combination or within-fold evaluation is the cheap check.

\subsection{Does classical static analysis do better?}\label{sec:static}
Lines added is a rhetorical baseline, not a deployable one: it measures size rather than structure and an agent optimising against it would simply write shorter patches. The fair version of the question is whether the signals a linter already computes rank silent failures where the critics do not. We built twelve language-agnostic features from the committed diff alone --- decision points and their density, maximum nesting, hunk count, files touched, net lines, error-handling and signature changes, comment ratio --- and, on Verified, six more from parsing each added hunk with Python's \texttt{ast} module, including whether the added code parses standing alone. All eight Multilingual languages get the lexical family; Verified is 100\% Python and gets both. Exploratory, and free.

The answer is no, in the same shape as everything else here. The strongest single feature on Verified is net lines added at 0.705 [0.51, 0.94], which edges out lines added (0.665) and is the only structural feature anywhere whose interval excludes chance; on Multilingual it falls to 0.536. A leave-one-repository-out logistic over the whole feature set reaches \textbf{0.644 [0.49, 0.78]} on Multilingual, the highest number any signal reaches on that benchmark, above the selector's 0.560 and the panel's 0.565 --- and the same model on Verified gives \textbf{0.436 [0.19, 0.60]}, worse than chance. The direction reverses across benchmarks, as the windowed statistic and the ruler did before it.

The endpoint tells the same story twice. Asked whether static analysis adds to the selector's own score, the leave-one-repository-out logistic reports $+0.169$ [$-0.01$, $+0.34$] on Multilingual --- against a baseline of 0.459 where the raw value is 0.560. That is the depressed-baseline signature of \S\ref{sec:endpoint} and \S\ref{sec:selector}, and applying this paper's own primary estimator instead gives $\mathbf{-0.086}$ [$-0.26$, $+0.08$]. On Verified the two estimators are $-0.061$ and $+0.083$. Nothing survives both benchmarks and both estimators.

We report this because it changes what the null is about. It would be convenient to conclude that language models are bad at judging their own work. What the data supports is narrower and less comfortable: on this population, neither an eight-model critic panel nor a linter's worth of structural features separates the trajectories that pass from the ones that do not. The signal may not be cheaply available to anything, which is a different claim, and a more useful one for anyone deciding where to spend an inference budget.

\subsection{Are the passes real? Label contamination and the endpoint}\label{sec:luckypass}
Our positive class is failure, so a trajectory that passes its tests without solving the task is a mislabelled \emph{negative}. That is the one labelling error that could hide a real signal from us, because it inflates the apparent separability of both $c$ and $u$; the literature on weak and lucky gold reports it as a live problem~\citep{wang2025solved,agentlens2026}. Adjudicating semantic correctness would need gold re-run with stronger tests, which we cannot afford, so we bound it instead with executed evidence already on disk.

The crude evasions are absent. No passing trajectory on either benchmark edited a file the reference test patch touches, and none edited any path matching a test-file convention. The harness restores test files before running, so tampering could not have worked, and nothing in our data suggests an agent tried. What remains is the subtler case: a patch that touches none of the files the reference patch touches and passes anyway. That is 0 of 22 passes on Verified and 4 of 42 on Multilingual, pooled 6.2\%. This is a proxy for the quantity that matters, not a measurement of it, so we read all four by hand against the issue text and the reference patch --- the judgement a file-level heuristic cannot make. \textbf{Three are unambiguous alternative fixes}, and in each the agent's own comments name the root cause the reference patch addresses. In \texttt{gohugoio/hugo} the reference escapes \texttt{\#} before URL parsing while the agent rewrote path escaping to preserve existing percent-encodings, both citing \texttt{url.Parse} treating \texttt{\#} as a fragment. In \texttt{immutable-js} the reference guards the slice factory while the agent fixed the index resolver one level below it, correctly diagnosing that a bitwise coercion was folding \texttt{NaN} to zero. In \texttt{micropython} both raise the same \texttt{TypeError} for duplicate keyword keys, at different call sites. \textbf{The fourth we cannot clear}: \texttt{vuejs/core} is a five-line change to subscriber notification order where the reference patch alters batching and pause semantics, and it may pass the tests without addressing what the reference fixed. So 6.2\% is the bound the automatic flag gives and \textbf{1.6\%}, one row in 64, is what inspection supports. The contrast with the failures runs the reassuring way: 10 of 35 Multilingual failures are disjoint from the reference patch against 4 of 42 passes, so touching the files the reference touches goes with passing, as it should if the flag tracks anything real.

The endpoint survives the worst case. Dropping the four flagged passes moves the pooled leave-one-repository-out $\DeltaAUROC$ from $-0.030$ to $-0.053$ [$-0.107$, $-0.012$] and the unfitted estimator from $+0.013$ to $-0.007$ [$-0.10$, $+0.10$]. Relabelling them as failures instead, the more punishing choice, gives $-0.024$ under both. $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$ falls from 0.542 to 0.516 and to 0.484 respectively. Nine of the ten scenario-by-estimator cells move the result further from the safeguard working; the exception moves 0.006 towards zero, from $-0.030$ to $-0.024$, and no cell becomes positive, so label contamination of the kind this literature documents cannot be what produced our null. We note the asymmetry honestly: this check can only rule contamination out as an explanation of a null, and could not have rescued a positive.

\subsection{A ruler beats the critics --- on one benchmark}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_ruler.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{On the pilot, two integers beat almost every model we ran.} Same 34 trajectories, same 12 positives. Gold bars need no model at all. Two bars exceed them, one critic's own score at full 16k (0.712) and the pruned-window $u_{\max}$ (0.686); both fell to chance on the replication. Exploratory (declared as such): the covariates were registered as controls, not baselines.}\label{fig:ruler}\end{figure}

Two integers, read off the diff and the transcript with no model at all, rank silent failures better than every reward model at 4k and every single-window disagreement statistic at any length (Fig.~\ref{fig:ruler}): lines added in the patch, 0.665; agent step count, 0.657. Bigger patches and longer trajectories fail more. The paired gap over the primary $u$ is $+0.16$ [$-0.11$, $+0.36$], exploratory, on twelve positives. Individual critics do cross this bar once they see the solution at length (\S\ref{sec:windows}); the panel's disagreement never does on a single window, and adds nothing to the ruler out of fold. We propose lines-added as the baseline any audit statistic must clear before it is worth a confirmatory run --- and note that on SWE-bench Verified, beating it by 0.10 in a paired test has power of about 0.3 at any $n$, because twelve repositories cap the effective sample (\S\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Mechanism: stable critics, uninformative disagreement}\label{sec:mechanism-e2}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_instability.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The same critic, the same trajectory, two different excerpts --- and almost unrelated scores.} The primary selecting critic's score on the trajectory prefix (x) against its score on two other windows of the same trajectory (y) --- because the windows contain different things (see Fig.~\ref{fig:stability}).}\label{fig:instab}\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[t]\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_stability.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{The critics are not noisy: they react to content.} Re-ask the same critic the same question and it answers the same way; change which part of the trajectory it reads and it does not. Each critic re-scored every Amendment-3 window after a content-preserving reformatting (headers re-cased, pruning marker reworded, one neutral line prepended) and after a 128-token shift. Spearman $r$ over 65--73 windows. The same critic on the same content is essentially deterministic; on adjacent or distant windows it is not.}\label{fig:stability}\end{figure}

Draft v0.1 of this paper read Fig.~\ref{fig:instab} --- the same reward model scoring the prefix and the patch of one trajectory with correlation 0.03--0.18 --- as evidence that these critics ``do not hold a judgment'' of code correctness. That reading could not distinguish critic noise from windows that legitimately contain different information, and we pre-registered a test to separate them (Amendment 4b, with the reading fixed in advance). The result withdraws the claim. Re-scoring the identical window after a reformatting that changes no information, every critic reproduces its ranking at $r=0.98$--$0.99$; after a 128-token shift, at $0.86$--$0.95$; the per-window panel $u$ itself at $0.91$ and $0.73$. Adjacent windows with 25\% overlap correlate at $0.20$--$0.56$, first against last at $-0.39$ to $0.47$. These critics are stable instruments responding to what is in front of them. The prefix score is a judgment of the problem; the patch score is a judgment of the patch; they differ because the inputs do.

The failure is therefore in the statistic, not the critics. A panel's disagreement is large when its members' rank-normalized scores spread out --- which they do for reasons that have nothing to do with correctness: different critics are calibrated to different aspects of a window, and the spread is dominated by that between-critic heterogeneity rather than by any shared uncertainty about the solution. The signal the critics carry individually (0.71--0.75 at length) is averaged away, not amplified, by measuring how much they disagree. Windowed $u_{\max}$ recovers some of it, plausibly because the maximum over windows behaves partly like a max over the critics' own low scores; its exploratory single-critic analogues (Fig.~\ref{fig:windows}, right) are consistent with that.

The proposal named a mechanism for when a panel should work --- errors that are not correlated --- and promised to test it by comparing quality-matched panels of differing error correlation. We can now report it. Forming all 35 four-member panels from the seven reward models that scored every candidate at all three lengths (the 32B has no 4k pass), and measuring for each its mean individual AUROC, the mean pairwise correlation of its members' errors, and its $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$, two things emerge. First, and more important: \textbf{there is no decorrelated panel to be had.} Every one of the 35 panels has pairwise error correlation between 0.71 and 0.89 on Verified and between 0.74 and 0.84 on Multilingual. The diversity this design assumes one can dial does not exist among off-the-shelf critics --- which is what the correlated-judge literature predicts~\citep{goel2025great,kim2025correlated,kohli2026nine}, and which we can now state as a measurement on this population rather than an expectation. Second, within that narrow range error correlation does not predict panel performance: the partial correlation with $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$, controlling for panel quality, is $-0.11$ on Verified and $+0.08$ on Multilingual, opposite in sign and both near zero.

A related check narrows the claim further. If the panel held information that disagreement merely failed to extract, a learned combination of its four raw scores should beat their spread. Out of fold and without fitted weights it does not: 0.43 against 0.50 on Verified, and 0.41 against 0.57 on Multilingual. On the committed population the panel is not carrying something that the wrong statistic discards; it is not carrying much at all.

This explains the earlier false positive too. On HumanEval+ with an LLM-as-judge critic, we briefly observed $u=0.69$ and a ``Goodhart signature'' of the critic below chance. Both were artifacts of a critic with three output values on three positives; with a real reward model selecting, both vanished. We withdrew the claim in the record before running the SWE-bench pilot, as we withdraw the instability claim here.

\section{Limitations and declared deviations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt]
\item \textbf{One agent; two selectors, both off the shelf.} gpt-5-mini at 40 steps throughout. The primary selector is a 4B chat reward model, so the primary selected population is that of a weak selector; Amendment 5 re-selects the same candidate pools with \texttt{gpt-5} and finds the same direction with a wider interval (\S\ref{sec:selector}). Neither is a deployed-grade trajectory verifier, and failure structure may differ for stronger agents or for a verifier trained on labelled failures.
\item \textbf{Selection did not concentrate failure here.} The unselected-candidate control (Amendment 4f) shows the committed candidate failing no more often than a passed-over one on either benchmark. The proposal's premise --- that selection \emph{enriches} the committed set for critic mistakes --- is therefore not demonstrated for this selector; what is demonstrated is that selection removes the selector's own signal (0.81 $\to$ 0.61 on Verified). A stronger selector could change both numbers.
\item \textbf{The selection-conditional regime.} With the primary 4B selector it is observed only on Verified (0.81 unselected to 0.61 committed); on Multilingual that critic had no signal in either arm. Amendment 5 re-selects with \texttt{gpt-5}, which does rank failures in the pool (0.71 on both benchmarks), but on the corrected population its signal is \emph{not} measurably consumed by selecting: the gap is 0.035 against a registered bar of 0.10, so S-P2 fails and this limitation is narrowed rather than closed (\S\ref{sec:selector}). What remains untested is a stronger \emph{agent}: \texttt{gpt-5-mini} fails 43.8\% of Verified instances against 24--30\% for the five stronger public runs on the same benchmark. A capable agent produces fewer failures, so holding 35 positives would need roughly 250 instances rather than 155 --- the study gets harder, not easier, and that is the next experiment.
\item \textbf{Critic classes, and a registered narrowing we decline.} Six chat-preference RMs, two code-trained RMs and five LLM judges from one vendor across two model generations; the 32B ran at 8k on most rows. Amendment 4c registered that if the best single LLM judge cleared 0.665 the paper's claim would narrow to \emph{reward-model} critics. It did clear it (0.752), and we do not narrow the claim, for a stated reason rather than by omission: the claim is about \emph{disagreement}, and judge disagreement failed its own bar on both benchmarks (0.528 and 0.514) and fell further with a newer judge generation (0.41). A capable judge's own score is a good detector; that is reported, and it is the opposite of the paper's thesis only if the thesis were about single critics, which it is not. Readers who prefer the registered narrowing should read every claim about disagreement as scoped to reward models, and the judge results as a same-direction extension. Cross-vendor judges and trained trajectory verifiers were not run; the claim is scoped to off-the-shelf critics.
\item \textbf{Gold is the harness verdict.} It accepts some incorrect patches and rejects some correct ones~\citep{wang2025solved,rajan2026auditing,agentlens2026}; failure rates are lower bounds and every AUROC is attenuated by label noise.
\item \textbf{A capable open-weight agent was tested; the axis ran; no critic qualified as its selector.} Answering a reviewer's request for capable open-weight agents, \texttt{DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash} was run on the same 41 Verified instances under the same scaffold, the same 40-step limit and the same harness gold, against gates registered in Amendment 10 before any trajectory existed and identical to those \texttt{gpt-6-luna} passed. It is decisively the more capable agent --- 30 of 41 resolved, an unconditional failure rate of 0.268 against \texttt{gpt-5-mini}'s 0.486, at \$0.0135 per trajectory --- yet it submitted a patch on only 32 of 41 against a registered bar of 80\%, failing the functioning gate by a single instance. All nine misses exhausted the step budget at 33--39 of 40 steps: none failed to parse, none crashed, and none was killed by the harness's 120-second command timeout, of which 15 occurred and were each survived. The binding constraint is convergence within a fixed budget, not the action format, which corrects the mechanism we inferred from earlier runs without gold. Raising the budget for one arm would destroy the comparability that makes the comparison meaningful, so the axis was not launched and no substitute was used (Amendment 10a \S1). \texttt{GLM-5.3}, run under the same 100-step, 16k-token settings for completeness, submitted on 16 of 41 and every one of those resolved, but 23 of 41 runs were cut by a \$0.25 per-trajectory cost cap the amendment had not named --- inert for DeepSeek at \$0.015 a run, binding for GLM at \$0.22 --- so the budget question is unanswered for GLM and it remains disqualified on cost at any budget. The axis then ran, under Amendment 13 (registered 1 October, run 1--3 October): 456 attempts over the registered 114 Multilingual instances, four candidates per instance at 100 steps, every candidate graded by the harness (365 of 456 resolved, 80.0\%, no harness errors, 24 of 24 oracle checks). \textbf{A7-P1} passed: DeepSeek's unconditional failure rate is 0.200 against 0.579 for \texttt{gpt-5-mini} at the same 100-step budget on the same instances, a gap of 0.379 on a registered bar of 0.10. But the Amendment 7a selector gate failed: the \texttt{gpt-5} judge's pool AUROC for detecting failure over the agent's 436 graded attempts is 0.552 against a bar of 0.65 (it was 0.671 on \texttt{gpt-5-mini}), and the five reward models scored on this axis sit between 0.419 and 0.523 --- every critic is at chance on this agent. The committed sets hold 17 failures (judge arm, $n=113$) and 19 (4B arm, $n=109$), under the registered floor of 20. \textbf{A7-P2} and \textbf{A7-P3} are therefore reported as \emph{descriptive only}: $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$ 0.544 [0.400, 0.697] and 0.502 [0.356, 0.649]; primary $\Delta$AUROC $+0.020$ [$-0.120$, $+0.149$] and $+0.032$ [$-0.079$, $+0.140$], the same shape as every other cell and with upper bounds far above $+0.05$, but no inference is drawn. The reading is not that the null extends to capable agents. It is sharper: on a capable agent the premise of critic selection fails first, because no instrument in this study separates its failures from its successes well enough to be a selector. The objection that the null is specific to weak agents is not retired and not narrowed by an equivalence bound; it is re-posed as a question about selectors. The run carried three recorded mid-run deviations and two addenda (a sandbox leak and a 50-second tunnel timeout in the scaffold's dependencies, two declared endpoint failures, six trajectories lost to the operator machine's network and regenerated, one ambiguous format error kept as a failure), none of which touched $c$, $u$ or gold; the registered readout and every input it reads are in the repository. Both open-weight agents failed a bar set by our own 40-step budget, so this is in part a finding about \emph{this scaffold} and not only about the models: DeepSeek resolved 30 of the 32 instances where it did submit, and a harness that spent fewer turns on whole-history searches might have carried it past 80\%. We report it as a capability result for this scaffold and do not generalise it to the models. Amendment 11 then tested that attribution directly. At a 100-step budget --- chosen after this outcome was visible and declared post hoc --- DeepSeek submitted on 39 of 41 (0.951 against the 0.80 bar), resolved 37, and no trajectory reached the ceiling (longest 79 steps), so the 40-step bar was the binding constraint. Re-measured at the same budget, \texttt{gpt-5-mini} resolved 16 of 41 with five runs that crashed the test suite on its own patch; counting those as failures its rate is 0.610 against DeepSeek's 0.098, excluding them 0.556, and the gap clears the 10-point bar either way. All three gates pass. The agent axis is therefore runnable on an open-weight agent under its own amendment, and anything it produces carries the post-hoc-budget caveat.

\item \textbf{Forty-seven positives.} Single-AUROC intervals are $\pm0.25$ (Verified) and $\pm0.14$ (Multilingual). Roughly twenty-five analyses were run on the twelve Verified positives without multiplicity correction; the replication is the correction: the configurations that cleared their bars on Verified did not clear them on Multilingual, while R-P1's registered CI-includes-chance conjunction is met as written but does not establish equivalence (upper bound 0.71 on $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$). The study supports failure to reject and an upper bound, not equivalence to chance; on the registered endpoint the bound is $+0.04$ pooled for the leave-one-repository-out logistic, $+0.12$ pooled across the three full-coverage estimators of it, and $+0.25$ on Verified alone (Table~\ref{tab:endpoint}); on single-protocol comparisons it is $+0.22$, and the widest interval in the sweep reaches $+0.63$, on the code-trained critic's 16k re-selection over eleven positives. We quote the loosest that applies to the population in question.
\item \textbf{Repository clustering.} Verified is 500 instances over 12 repositories (django 46\%); from six public submissions the ICC of resolution by repository is 0.033--0.053, an effective $n$ of 64--95 of 500 (Fig.~\ref{fig:pilot}). Clustered bootstraps with nine clusters are anti-conservative; we report null-calibrated coverage in the record. The paired ``beat the ruler by 0.10'' test has power $\approx$0.3 on Verified at any $n$, which is why the replication changed repositories rather than instances.
\item \textbf{A corrected defect in Amendment 5.} The selector-axis analysis originally filtered its population by the 4B-committed candidate's agent-declared-complete flag rather than the \texttt{gpt-5}-committed candidate's. Corrected, Multilingual gains 16 eligible instances (85 to 101 rows, 32 to 44 failures); S-P2 moves from pass to fail, $\mathrm{AUROC}(u)$ from 0.614 to 0.591, and the leave-one-repository-out endpoint from $+0.375$ with a collapsed baseline to $+0.012$ with a sound one. Verified is unaffected, as is Amendment 2. The correction is dated in the pre-registration and the regenerating script is released; it changes registered verdicts, so it is declared here rather than folded silently into the numbers.
\item \textbf{Exploratory analyses.} The diversity-versus-error-correlation comparison, the learned-combiner check, judge calibration, selection pressure at $n{=}2$ versus $n{=}4$, cross-benchmark transfer, agent self-verification as a feature, a margin-stratified check, the failure taxonomy of \S\ref{sec:taxonomy}, the label-contamination bound of \S\ref{sec:luckypass} and the static-analysis baseline of \S\ref{sec:static} were all run after the confirmatory results and are reported as exploratory, without multiplicity correction.
\item \textbf{Input length and windows.} The primary scored a 4k prefix; the six chat RMs were scored at a true 8k and 16k; \texttt{AceCodeRM-7B} fell back to 4k on 153/164 rows. Pruning left most trajectories at one to three windows; $u_{\max}$ is a maximum of noisy statistics and biased upward under the null.
\item \textbf{Declared deviations.} 41 instances, not 50 (generation stalled on host disk; the missing nine were set by list order). Amendment 4a's registered difficulty control was a near-oracle (0.95) and was replaced by a labelled structure-only base. The replication's \$15 generation cap was exceeded (\$18.30; the account's rate limit killed 138 partial trajectories) because stopping at 420/456 would have dropped the harder instances first. 26\% of replication candidates submitted no patch; 7 committed patches were ungradable and excluded.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\paragraph{Best-of-$n$, Goodhart and the winner's curse.} Selecting the argmax of a learned proxy over-optimizes it~\citep{gao2023scaling,beirami2024theoretical}; at inference time the effect appears as reward hacking that grows with $n$~\citep{khalaf2025inference,stroebl2024inference,brown2024monkeys} and can be mitigated by pessimism~\citep{yu2026caution}. The statistical form is the optimizer's curse~\citep{smith2006optimizer}; selecting on a noisy score and then studying the selected set is selection bias in the structural sense~\citep{hernan2004structural}. Two recent notes make the evaluation consequence explicit: success counted before an answer is chosen is not deployed success~\citep{emadi2026scaling}, and best-of-$n$ degrades calibration even under a perfect ranker~\citep{trilemma2026}. Our estimand is this consequence for auditors.

\paragraph{Reward-model uncertainty and ensembles.} Ensembles mitigate but do not eliminate over-optimization~\citep{eisenstein2023helping,coste2023reward}; uncertainty-aware reward models~\citep{lou2024urm} and calibrated process reward models~\citep{park2025calibration} change \emph{selection}. We ask a different question --- given the trajectory the selector already committed, does a disjoint panel's disagreement carry residual information --- which none of these evaluate.

\paragraph{Correlated judges and panel effective size.} Capable models make correlated errors~\citep{goel2025great,kim2025correlated}; judge panels supply few effective votes~\citep{kohli2026nine,hossain2026agreement}; common-mode error is not identifiable from internal scores~\citep{afrin2026ensembles}; consensus can rival trained verifiers where errors are decorrelated~\citep{verga2024juries,liu2026jury} and disagreement can be structured into a confidence estimator~\citep{jiang2026discouq}. On code, verification helps only on margin-pivotal cases and a single round of debate erases the disagreement it needs~\citep{shu2026pivotal,shu2026unanimity}. These predict our null; we supply an executed-gold instance on selected agent trajectories.

\paragraph{Process and outcome verifiers.} Step-level supervision~\citep{lightman2023verify,wang2024mathshepherd}, generative verifiers~\citep{zhang2024genrm} and agent PRMs~\citep{xi2025agentprm} score and select; they are not evaluated as residual auditors of a selected set.

\paragraph{SWE agents, verifiers and gold.} SWE-bench and its Verified and Multilingual subsets~\citep{jimenez2024swebench,swebenchverified2024,yang2025multilingual}, with mini-SWE-agent as the reference scaffold~\citep{miniswe2025}, are our testbed; SWE-smith, SWE-Gym and R2E-Gym supply training data and verifiers~\citep{yang2025swesmith,pan2024swegym,jain2025r2egym}; Agentless questions the need for agency at all~\citep{xia2024agentless}. Execution-free critics and reward models for SWE trajectories~\citep{yadavally2025critics,shum2025swerm} are evaluated on discrimination, calibration and test-time scaling over candidate pools; trained failure locators and prefix monitors~\citep{rahman2026scout,wang2026failfast} and sequential-testing verifiers~\citep{sadhuka2025evaluator} target failure directly. None conditions on an upstream selector. The gold itself is imperfect: accepted patches can fail developer tests, test suites can be weak, and passing runs can be lucky~\citep{wang2025solved,rajan2026auditing,agentlens2026,wang2026horizon}.

\paragraph{Agent failure and self-uncertainty.} Agents declare success falsely~\citep{advani2026silent}, are overconfident about their own success~\citep{kaddour2026agentic}, and their early uncertainty does not predict failure~\citep{li2026laststep}; online auditors have been trained for multi-agent settings~\citep{zhang2026agentforesight}. Semantic entropy and self-knowledge~\citep{farquhar2024semantic,kadavath2022know} and selective classification~\citep{geifman2017selective} are the uncertainty tools these build on; long-context position effects~\citep{liu2024lost} bear on what a critic sees in a window.

\paragraph{Reward models used.} Skywork-Reward-V2~\citep{liu2025skywork2}, GRM~\citep{yang2024grm} and AceCodeRM~\citep{zeng2025acecoder}, chosen by RewardBench standing~\citep{lambert2024rewardbench,malik2025rewardbench2}.

\paragraph{Methodology.} Paired AUROC comparison for nested models should not use the DeLong test~\citep{demler2012misuse,pepe2013testing}; clustered ROC methods~\citep{obuchowski1997nonparametric} and decision curves~\citep{vickers2006decision} inform our analysis. The pre-registration trail follows the registered-report tradition~\citep{nosek2018preregistration,chambers2022registered}.

\section{Conclusion}
A third to a half of what a reward-model critic confidently committed on two SWE benchmarks was broken. The proposed safeguard --- disagreement among a disjoint panel of off-the-shelf critics --- did not yield a replicable failure-detection signal under any configuration we pre-registered and ran, across reward models and LLM judges alike, on forty-six repositories. The claim this study can carry is a bound, not an absence: with forty-seven positives it fails to reject and puts the incremental value of disagreement below $+0.12$ on the pooled population. Establishing equivalence would need roughly five times the failures, and a more capable agent produces fewer of them, so that is a more expensive study than this one, not a cheaper one. One configuration crossed its bar on the pilot: the maximum disagreement over pruned windows reached 0.686 on twelve positives, with a free permutation $p$ of 0.04 that rose to 0.21 within repository. It did not survive replication. The critics are not noisy: they are stable, content-driven, and given the solution at length the best frontier judge ranks silent failures at 0.78--0.83. In these samples, their \emph{disagreement} did not reveal the missing information. The point estimates that suggested otherwise on twelve positives were settled the way they needed to be, on thirty-seven fresh repositories rather than five hundred more trajectories, and they are at chance. For trajectories from this single agent, anyone building an escalation trigger for critic-selected agents should evaluate it where it will be used --- on the selected population, against the selector's own score, with executed gold --- and should expect off-the-shelf disagreement to add nothing detectable under the primary selector: on the registered endpoint, no estimator of it separates from zero, and the loosest bound we can defend on the pooled population is $+0.12$ AUROC over the selector's own score. The stronger-selector cell is consistent with that null and does not establish it; a stronger agent remains the open axis.

\section{How the claim changed under scrutiny}\label{sec:history}
\footnotesize
The analysis was pre-registered; the write-up was not, and a negative result is easy to reverse-engineer once the data is in. This appendix is the audit trail. Every draft of this paper is a commit in the released repository, and the table records what each one changed about the claim rather than about the prose.

Two features of it are worth stating, because they are the reason we offer it. The direction of travel is monotone: every revision narrowed the claim, weakened a number, or withdrew one, and none strengthened one. And the two corrections that mattered most were self-inflicted rather than requested. The mechanism announced in v0.1 was refuted by our own test--retest data in v0.2, before any reviewer saw it. The single-estimator bound reported in v0.5 was replaced in v0.6 by four estimators because we could not defend relying on one, and that decision is what made the Amendment 5 anomaly visible two days later, where a single estimator would have reversed the paper's conclusion. We first attributed that anomaly to the estimator; a later audit found the real cause was a defect in the population, and the entry for v0.9.3 records it. Readers should discount the claim for the fact that its author chose the analyses; they can at least see which way the choices went.
